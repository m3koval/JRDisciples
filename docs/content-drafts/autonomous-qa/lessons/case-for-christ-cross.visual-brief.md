# Visual Learning Pack Brief: case-for-christ-cross

Lane: lessons  
Title: Why Did Jesus Have to Die?  
Anchors: Romans 5:6-11; 1 Peter 3:18; Isaiah 53:5-6  
Gate: PASS

## Scope

Create a visual learning pack strategy for exactly this lesson. Do not generate images in this stage. Do not publish.

Lesson focus: help children understand sin, substitution, justice, mercy, costly love, forgiveness, and peace with God through Jesus in a warm, reverent, child-safe way.

Visual tone: serious but hopeful, gentle, worshipful, clear, and non-manipulative. The cross should be visually central as a symbol of God’s justice and mercy meeting, but never graphic or fear-driven.

## Character Canon Strategy

Use polished 3D animated human children, not minifigs.

Recurring cast block for prompt consistency:

Michael is a 9–10-year-old polished 3D animated human boy with child proportions, curly brown hair, warm tan skin, a green adventure tunic/outfit, and a small explorer satchel or lantern; he is the steady leader, brave but humble. Rosie is an 8–9-year-old polished 3D animated human girl with child proportions, wavy brown hair, a purple adventure dress/tunic, and a small Bible book or Scripture-light object; she is gentle, thoughtful, and truth-centered. Joseph is an 8–9-year-old polished 3D animated human boy with child proportions, dark curly hair, a blue adventure outfit, and a small notebook or tool pouch; he is curious, energetic, kind, and teachable. Gracie is a 6–7-year-old polished 3D animated human girl with younger child proportions, warm brown skin, black curly hair in two puff ponytails or short curls, and a yellow rain hood/rain cloak in storm scenes; she is bright, trusting, and brave in a younger-child way.

Consistency rule: keep each child the same age, height relationship, face shape, hair, skin tone, and clothing colors across every image. Only include characters named for the scene. Toy-brick / voxel styling may apply to environments and props, not the children.

Recommended cast usage:
- Hero: Michael, Rosie, and Joseph as the main investigation/adventure trio; optionally Gracie if the composition needs a younger-child response of trust, but avoid overcrowding.
- Bible truth/story truth: use symbolic diorama panels first; if children appear, place them as observers outside the salvation-event symbols.
- Artifact/history: usually no children or only small scale observers, so the reconstruction remains clear.
- Activity/challenge: all four children may appear around the tabletop activity because this slot is practical and relational.
- Thumbnail: use 1-3 recognizable faces/silhouettes maximum for readability.

## Global Prompt Strategy

Preferred style phrase:

> high-quality children’s Bible adventure illustration, polished 3D animated human children, blocky toy-brick / voxel diorama environment, open glowing Bible as miniature landscape, warm dawn light, reverent hopeful atmosphere, child-safe gospel lesson about the cross, justice and mercy, no readable text

Global negatives:

> no readable text, no fake Bible words, no logos, no watermarks, no gore, no blood, no nails, no wounds, no graphic crucifixion, no Jesus suffering close-up, no horror, no fear pressure, no angry Father/unwilling Son imagery, no minifig children, no aging up or recasting characters, no shaming children, no generated captions or Bible verses inside the image

Render Bible references, hard-word labels, card labels, titles, and captions in site UI/HTML, not inside generated images.

## Required Visual Slots

### 1. Hero Visual — “The Crossroads of Justice and Mercy”

Concept: Michael, Rosie, and Joseph stand on an open glowing Bible that becomes a warm miniature landscape. Two gentle light paths labeled in UI only as Justice and Mercy converge toward a simple distant cross silhouette on a sunrise hill; the path continues toward a welcoming home/family-light motif representing peace with God.

Purpose: Introduce the big question with awe and hope: the cross is not defeat, panic, or fear pressure; it is God’s united rescue plan.

Prompt notes:
- Include Michael with lantern/satchel, Rosie with small Bible/Scripture-light object, Joseph with notebook/tool pouch.
- Show the cross as a distant, clean silhouette with golden light, not a crucifixion scene.
- Use a broken-then-restored path/bridge motif to suggest sin breaks fellowship and Jesus brings us to God.
- No readable path signs inside the generated art; add Justice/Mercy/Peace UI overlays separately if desired.

UI text/caption suggestion:
- “At the cross, God showed perfect justice and deep mercy so sinners can have peace with God.”

### 2. Bible Truth / Story Truth Visual — “The Righteous One for the Unrighteous”

Concept: A symbolic open-Bible diorama with three clear stations: a broken window/path stone for sin being serious, a pure golden substitute shield/heart standing between danger and a small paper-person symbol, and a restored bridge/homeward path glowing with peace.

Purpose: Teach 1 Peter 3:18 and Romans 5:6-11 visually: Jesus, the righteous One, willingly stood in the place of sinners to bring them to God.

Prompt notes:
- Use symbolic gospel objects rather than a graphic Passion scene.
- A simple empty cross silhouette may appear in the background, but do not show Jesus’ body or suffering.
- Keep the theological direction clear: substitution leads to forgiveness and restored relationship, not “try harder.”
- Avoid courtroom imagery that feels cold or punitive for younger children; if using justice scales, balance them with warm mercy light and family-home imagery.

UI text/caption suggestion:
- “Jesus never sinned. He took the place of sinners to bring us to God.”

### 3. Artifact / History Visual — “First-Century Crucifixion Context, Child-Safe Reconstruction”

Concept: Original historically informed reconstruction panel showing non-graphic Roman-era context: a distant execution hill silhouette outside a city wall, rough wooden beams prepared off to the side, a Roman road, and a simple map/table of neutral objects such as coin, sealed order tablet, and stone architecture. The cross remains distant and empty.

Purpose: Give historical setting for why “cross” meant real death in the Roman world without dwelling on violence or implying an artifact proves the atonement.

Required label:
- “Original child-safe reconstruction of first-century Roman crucifixion context; not a photograph and not a graphic depiction.”

Historical notes:
- Roman crucifixion was a real public execution method; for this age group, state that it was serious and painful without visual details.
- If later referencing artifacts such as the Yehohanan heel bone, Pilate Stone, Roman nails, or first-century Jerusalem sites, treat them as context for the world of the New Testament, not as proof of salvation doctrine.
- Prefer original reconstructions over real artifact photos unless exact open-license/public-domain status is verified.

Concrete artifact/place options for this slot:
- Pilate Stone / Pontius Pilate inscription: context for Pilate as a real Roman official in Judea. Use a verified open-license photo only, or create an original simplified stone-inscription drawing labeled “based on the Pilate inscription.”
- Caesarea Maritima ruins / Roman theater: context for Roman administration in Judea. Use open-license/public-domain images only, with attribution captured.
- Second Temple Jerusalem map: create an original simplified “approximate” map showing Temple Mount, city walls, and outside-the-wall execution/burial context. Do not trace copyrighted study Bible maps.
- Great Isaiah Scroll / Qumran context: helpful for Isaiah 53 as ancient Scripture context. Because scroll images often have restrictions, prefer an original parchment-scroll illustration or verified open-license Qumran cave photo.
- 1st-century Jewish rock-cut tomb examples: use only as background context; label clearly as “example of a first-century tomb,” not “the tomb of Jesus.”
- Yehohanan crucifixion evidence: teacher-background only; do not include graphic bone/nail imagery in the children’s pack.

License cautions:
- Real historical photos must be verified open-license/public-domain before use; capture source URL, license, attribution, and derivative/commercial-use terms.
- Good starting points: Wikimedia Commons with exact license verification, The Metropolitan Museum of Art Open Access for ancient coins/objects, Library of Congress or David Rumsey for public-domain/reference maps.
- Do not use museum, tourism, encyclopedia, commercial Bible-photo, study-Bible-map, or Google-result images without verified reuse rights.
- CC BY-SA images may create share-alike obligations; confirm compatibility before selecting them.
- Avoid public-domain Passion paintings if they are graphic, adult-coded, dark, or centered on wounds.

### 4. Activity / Challenge Visual — “Justice and Mercy Cards”

Concept: A tabletop/open-Bible activity scene with Michael, Rosie, Joseph, and Gracie arranging two blank index cards, a small paper person, and a paper heart. Golden light from the open Bible connects the cards to a simple cross symbol and restored path.

Purpose: Support the lesson activity “The Two Cards: Justice and Mercy” without baking labels into generated art.

Prompt notes:
- Cards must be blank in the generated image.
- UI/HTML should add labels: Justice, Mercy, Sin, Substitute, Peace with God, if needed.
- Children’s expressions should be thoughtful, safe, and encouraged; no guilt panic or shame.
- Gracie may be included here as a younger child placing the heart near both cards, reinforcing trust and tenderness.

Challenge UI idea:
- “Explain each word in one sentence: sin, justice, mercy, grace, substitute.”

### 5. Thumbnail Visual — “Why Did Jesus Have to Die?”

Concept: High-readability thumbnail with open glowing Bible, warm sunrise cross silhouette, two converging light beams for justice and mercy, and one or two Junior Disciples faces looking reverent and hopeful.

Purpose: Invite clicks with clarity and warmth, not shock or sensationalism.

Prompt notes:
- Strong simple shapes: cross silhouette, glowing Bible, warm gold/blue contrast.
- No small generated text; title should be overlaid in site UI.
- Avoid blood-red color palettes; prefer dawn gold, soft white, and calm blue/purple shadows.
- Do not show Jesus’ face/body, nails, blood, thorns, or wounds.

## Optional Short Video Concepts

1. “Justice and Mercy Meet” — 6-8 seconds. Camera glides over an open Bible as two light paths converge at a distant empty cross silhouette, then continue toward a warm home/peace glow. UI overlays add: Justice, Mercy, Peace with God.
2. “The Substitute Shield” — 6 seconds. A symbolic golden shield/heart gently steps between a broken path and a paper-person figure, then the bridge repairs with light. UI caption: “Jesus took the place of sinners.”
3. “The Two Cards Activity” — 6-8 seconds. Blank cards slide into place on a tabletop, a paper heart lands between them, and the children smile thoughtfully. UI labels added outside the generated video.
4. “Cross and Resurrection Belong Together” — 8 seconds. Start with a distant empty cross at dusk, pan across an open Bible to sunrise and an empty tomb silhouette. No Jesus depiction; UI caption: “Jesus died for our sins and rose again.”
5. “From Guilt to Peace” — 6 seconds. A broken stone path on Bible pages is repaired by warm light leading toward a welcoming doorway. Keep symbolic, gentle, and non-shaming.

## Risks to Avoid

- Graphic Passion imagery: blood, nails, wounds, thorns close-ups, suffering faces, weapons, or realistic violence.
- Fear-pressure visuals that try to make children feel personally crushed, panicked, or hopeless.
- Theological miscue: portraying the Father as angry at an unwilling Jesus. Maintain united rescue plan: the Father sent the Son; the Son willingly gave Himself in love.
- Making sin seem like a mere accident or, conversely, making children feel uniquely blamed for Jesus’ death.
- Generated readable Bible text, labels, title cards, fake inscriptions, or warped captions.
- Courtroom-only visuals that make God seem cold; pair justice with mercy, love, and restored relationship.
- Depicting Jesus directly in graphic suffering; safest route is symbolic empty/distant cross imagery.
- Recasting, aging, or changing the Junior Disciples children, or turning them into toy-brick/minifig characters.
- Using historical photos/artworks without verified open license/public-domain status.
- Overstating artifacts or history as proving atonement; visuals can give context, while Scripture supplies the message.
